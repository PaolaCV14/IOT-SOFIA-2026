	\begin{abstract}
		Modular robotic systems leverage repeatable electromechanical units to enable flexible, scalable, and reconfigurable architectures. In interactive human-robot installations, spatial tracking of human body posture and gestures provides intuitive stimulus-response dynamics. \linelabel{ln:abs}This paper presents the modular design and experimental validation of key subsystems for SOFIA, a planned interactive kinetic surface comprising sixteen electromechanical units, each providing two rotational degrees of freedom actuated by commercial MG90S servomotors. While commercial servomotors encapsulate internal closed-loop position control via pulse-width modulation (PWM), persistent angular errors caused by mechanical stalls, external disturbances, or fabrication tolerances induce thermal degradation and excessive power consumption. \linelabel{ln:abs-inv}To investigate actuator reliability under persistent position error conditions, this work formulates an experimental characterization protocol assessing the thermal response of an MG90S under mechanical stall. Using an LM35 precision temperature sensor and a commanded reference angle, actuator heating is characterized during continuous stall, \linelabel{ln:abs-tau}revealing an initial quasi-linear heating rate of $\beta \approx 0.214\,^\circ\text{C/s}$ ($R^2 = 0.993$) and casing temperatures reaching $70.21^\circ\text{C}$ across extended testing before thermal equilibrium is attained. In parallel, a real-time 3D visual tracking pipeline and a 16-module kinematic simulation are implemented independently; \linelabel{ln:abs-claim}together with the empirical heating dynamics, these subsystems provide a first estimate of the operational limits and thermal constraints intended to guide the proposed end-to-end physical integration of the kinetic surface.
	\end{abstract}

	\section{Introduction}
	\label{sec:introduction}
	
	\IEEEPARstart{M}{odular} \linelabel{ln:intro1}robotics enables constructing complex spatial mechanisms from repeatable functional units integrating mechanical, electronic, and control capabilities, offering superior reconfigurability and maintainability over monolithic robotic architectures~\cite{seo2019modular}.
	
	\linelabel{ln:intro-hri}In parallel, socially interactive and human-robot interaction (HRI) systems incorporate perception and actuation mechanisms to establish continuous, non-verbal dialogue with human participants. Within this domain, posture, gestures, and body kinematics constitute vital communication channels for interpreting user intent and synthesizing reactive robotic behavior~\cite{fong2003social}. Consequently, modern kinetic robotic installations operate not merely as automated spatial mechanisms, but as interactive expressive surfaces capable of responding synchronously to multimodal human stimuli.
	
	\linelabel{ln:intro-sofia}Within this framework, this paper introduces SOFIA, a modular robotic installation comprising sixteen electromechanical units arranged in a kinetic honeycomb array. Each module provides two rotational degrees of freedom (2-DOF) driven by dual metal-gear MG90S servomotors (32 actuators total). \linelabel{ln:intro-onboard}Actuator control and signal distribution are organized through a centralized electronic architecture, wherein a master ESP32-C3 processing node manages trajectory interpolation and commands dual 16-channel PCA9685 PWM drivers over an I$^2$C bus to synchronously synthesize the 32 servo control pulses. Supervisory communication is established via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) and Wi-Fi, with hardware provision for inter-module expansion over an industrial RS-485 / Modbus bus.
	
	\linelabel{ln:intro-hw}The system relies on the ESP32-C3 microcontroller as the core processing and telemetry engine~\cite{espressif2024esp32c3}, coupled with PCA9685 16-channel 12-bit I$^2$C PWM controllers~\cite{nxp2015pca9685} to synthesize precise 50~Hz control pulses for the servomotors. Although commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) servomotors simplify spatial positioning, their internal closed-loop controllers remain completely encapsulated within the actuator housing. Consequently, while the supervisory system commands an angular setpoint via external 50~Hz PWM duty cycles, the internal drive strategy utilized to eliminate error between reference and actual shaft position is inaccessible to the designer.
	
	\linelabel{ln:intro-stall}This characteristic becomes critical when a servomotor experiences operational constraints where it cannot reach the commanded angular setpoint. In large-scale kinetic arrays composed of numerous densely packed modules, mechanical interference, fabrication tolerances, structural interference, or physical obstacles produce persistent, non-zero position errors. Under such conditions, internal driver behavior governs current draw, internal thermal dissipation, and long-term hardware reliability.
	
	\linelabel{ln:intro-bb}A classical control archetype relevant to bounded-input actuators under persistent saturation is bang-bang (on-off) control. \linelabel{ln:intro-boyd}Under Pontryagin's maximum principle, bang-bang switching strategies naturally arise in optimal control problems subject to bounded actuation limits, providing a theoretical archetype for analyzing actuator behavior under persistent error~\cite{kirk2004optimal}. However, because both bang-bang switching and saturated proportional control deliver peak voltage during mechanical stall, a thermal trajectory alone cannot discriminate between them without direct current telemetry under varying error amplitudes.
	
	\linelabel{ln:intro-obj}To address this challenge, this paper experimentally characterizes the electromechanical and thermal behavior of the MG90S servomotor under persistent position error. By imposing a mechanical constraint on the output shaft and monitoring surface temperature with a precision LM35 sensor, this study extracts empirical first-order thermal models via linear regression. \linelabel{ln:hyp}The primary working hypothesis posits that persistent angular error induces an observable thermal signature that quantitatively reflects actuator operational constraints. \linelabel{ln:rq}Accordingly, the central research question guiding this work is: \textit{Does persistent position error in an MG90S servomotor produce an identifiable thermal signature that bounds the safe holding time of a SOFIA kinetic module?} To answer this question, four specific, measurable technical objectives are addressed:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
		\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
		\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
		\item \linelabel{ln:obj-act}\textbf{Actuator Evaluation:} Quantify angular limits ($\theta \in [19.3^\circ, 70.2^\circ]$) and settling times ($t_s \in [75, 180]\text{ ms}$) under 50~Hz PWM drive.
		\item \linelabel{ln:obj-therm}\textbf{Thermal Modeling:} Characterize stall heating to determine empirical slope ($\beta \approx 0.214\,^\circ\text{C/s}$) and safety limits ($>65^\circ\text{C}$).
		\item \linelabel{ln:obj-vis}\textbf{Perception Validation:} Extract 3D pose and 21-point hand landmarks via MediaPipe at $\ge 30$~FPS to arbitrate dynamic gestures.
		\item \linelabel{ln:obj-kin}\textbf{Kinematic Simulation:} Formulate 2-DOF analytical kinematics and validate 16-module spatial tracking in dynamic simulation.
	\end{enumerate}
	\linelabel{ln:contrib}The core contribution of this work lies in combining a vision-driven modular robotic architecture for human-robot interaction with rigorous experimental actuator characterization, establishing thermal boundaries \linelabel{ln:contrib-claim}intended to support operational reliability in kinetic surfaces.
	
	\subsection{Related Work}
	\label{sec:related_work}
	
	\linelabel{ln:rw1}Recent advances in interactive surfaces and vision-driven robotics have accelerated with lightweight machine learning inference frameworks. Qui{\~n}onez \textit{et al.}~\cite{quinonez2022mediapipe} and Lugaresi \textit{et al.}~\cite{lugaresi2019mediapipe} demonstrated the efficacy of MediaPipe perception graphs for real-time 3D landmark estimation, which, combined with optimized computer vision pipelines via OpenCV~\cite{bradski2000opencv}, enable 21-point hand skeleton topologies to be extracted with minimal computational latency.
	
	\linelabel{ln:rw2}In the domain of dynamic physical interfaces and shape displays, Follmer \textit{et al.}~\cite{follmer2013inform} introduced inFORM, a 2.5D shape display comprising 900 motorized linear pins operating at high update rates for dynamic tangible interaction. Expanding toward interactive kinetic architectural furniture, Nakagaki \textit{et al.}~\cite{nakagaki2016transform} developed Transform, incorporating over 1,000 independent motorized linkages. In modular and reconfigurable robotics, Seo \textit{et al.}~\cite{seo2019modular} formalized scalability criteria, distributed communication, and power distribution across repeatable electromechanical units, while Fong \textit{et al.}~\cite{fong2003social} surveyed social robotics, demonstrating that physical motion, non-verbal mirroring, and gestural responsiveness enhance user engagement.

	\linelabel{ln:demo-intro}During the initial \textit{Discover} stage of the D-Lab methodology, guided physical observation of the SOFIA proof-of-concept module identified critical operational failure modes emerging from dense actuator packaging. The modular kinetic cell decomposes into five core functional subsystems, summarized in the functional decomposition matrix (Table~\ref{tab:functional_matrix}).
	
	\linelabel{ln:demo-obs}Guided laboratory observations revealed that during spatial multi-facet alignment, adjacent 3D-printed petals occasionally encounter mechanical interference due to fabrication tolerances ($\pm 0.4\text{ mm}$) and rapid user gesture transients. Consequently, actuators become physically constrained before achieving commanded setpoints. 
	
	\linelabel{ln:demo-tree}To rigorously structure this vulnerability, a problem tree analysis was synthesized:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Direct Effects:} Armature coil burnout, PLA housing softening ($T_g \approx 60^\circ\text{C}$), power supply rail voltage sags, and complete module desynchronization.
		\item \textbf{Core Focal Problem:} \textit{Persistent angular tracking error} ($e(t) = \theta_d(t) - \theta_{\text{stall}} \neq 0$) occurring across densely packed, unmonitored COTS actuators.
		\item \textbf{Root Causes:} (i) lack of direct shaft position telemetry to the supervisory controller, (ii) aggressive internal analog/digital H-bridge driver compensation under stall, and (iii) absence of thermal dissipation pathways in enclosed modular housings.
	\end{itemize}
	These Discover findings motivated the experimental thermal characterization and bounded control strategies developed in this work.
	
	\subsection{Formal Problem Formulation}
	\label{sec:problem_formulation}
	
	\linelabel{ln:pf}The SOFIA system requires coordinated spatial orientation of 32 independent servomotors across 16 electromechanical modules. Each actuator receives an angular reference setpoint $\theta_d(t)$ and is commanded to achieve the target physical orientation $\theta(t)$.
	
	\linelabel{ln:pf-e}The angular position error $e(t)$ for any given actuator is formally defined as:
	\begin{equation}
		e(t) = \theta_d(t) - \theta(t),
		\label{eq:position_error}
	\end{equation}
	where $\theta_d(t) \in [\theta_{\min}, \theta_{\max}]$ corresponds to the desired (commanded) angular position, and $\theta(t)$ denotes the instantaneous actual angular position of the output shaft. In the single-axis experimental characterization, the scalar variable $\theta(t) \equiv q_j(t)$ ($j \in \{1, 2\}$) denotes the coordinate of the servomotor under test, corresponding identically to either joint axis of the 2-DOF kinetic module.
	
	\linelabel{ln:pf-ts}Under ideal, unconstrained operation, the internal feedback loop drives $e(t) \to 0$ within a settling time $t_s \in [75, 180]\text{ ms}$. However, when an external mechanical constraint prevents the actuator from reaching $\theta_d(t)$ such that $\theta(t) = \theta_{\text{stall}} \neq \theta_d(t)$, the error persists indefinitely:
	\begin{equation}
		e(t) = \theta_d(t) - \theta_{\text{stall}} = \Delta\theta_0 \neq 0, \quad \forall t \ge t_0.
		\label{eq:persistent_error}
	\end{equation}
	
	When $e(t)$ \linelabel{ln:pf-hbridge}persists, the internal H-bridge driver continuously supplies high current to the DC coreless motor in an attempt to minimize the tracking discrepancy. This condition causes significant Joule heating ($P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R$) within the motor armature and driver silicon, inducing rapid thermal accumulation. Modeling this thermal profile and its associated time constant provides the quantitative foundation for actuator risk mitigation in the SOFIA array.

	\subsection{Experimental Setup: Thermal Characterization under Persistent Error}
	\label{sec:thermal_experiment}
	
	\linelabel{ln:setup}To investigate the thermal behavior and reliability limits of the MG90S servomotors within the SOFIA architecture, an experimental testing apparatus was designed to record thermal dissipation under mechanical restriction. The test bench comprises:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \linelabel{ln:setup-5v}An MG90S metal-gear servomotor powered by a regulated 5.0~V DC supply.
		\item A 10~k$\Omega$ linear precision potentiometer utilized to set and modulate the commanded angular setpoint $\theta_{\text{ref}}(t)$.
		\item \linelabel{ln:setup-lm35}A calibrated LM35 precision centigrade temperature sensor mechanically thermally coupled to the servomotor casing via thermal interface compound, providing an analog output of $10\text{ mV}/^\circ\text{C}$.
		\item \linelabel{ln:setup-daq}An acquisition node based on an ESP32 microcontroller acquiring angular commands and surface temperature $T_{\text{servo}}(t)$ at a sampling rate of $1\text{ Hz}$.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\linelabel{ln:setup-stop}A rigid mechanical boundary stop was rigidly fixed to the servomotor output horn to physically prevent the output shaft from reaching the commanded angular reference $\theta_{\text{ref}}(t)$, deliberately maintaining a persistent non-zero error condition: $e(t) = \theta_{\text{ref}}(t) - \theta_{\text{stall}} > 0$. Ambient room temperature $T_{\text{amb}}$ was monitored as the reference operating baseline.
	
	\linelabel{ln:trials}The experimental characterization protocol was conducted in two sequential stages:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Trial 1 (Failure and Degradation Assessment):} \linelabel{ln:trial1}The actuator was initialized at $T_{\text{amb}} = 21.37^\circ\text{C}$ and \linelabel{ln:trial1-180}subjected to a persistent stall reference of $\theta_{\text{ref}} = 180^\circ$. The test proceeded continuously for $226\text{ s}$, until distinct audible abnormalities, gear strain, and subsequent actuator degradation occurred. The surface temperature climbed monotonically to a critical peak of $T_{\max} = 64.84^\circ\text{C}$.
		\item \textbf{Trial 2 (Extended Thermal Accumulation and Cooling):} \linelabel{ln:trial2}In an extended continuous run spanning elapsed time $t \in [0, 681]\text{ s}$ (corresponding to DAQ timestamps $t \in [973.1, 1654.1]\text{ s}$, $N = 682$), the actuator thermal dynamics were characterized under persistent locked-rotor conditions. The output horn was constrained by the mechanical boundary stop at $\theta_{\text{stall}} \approx 0^\circ$ while commanded steps of $\theta_{\text{ref}} \in \{45^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ\}$ were applied to maintain $e(t) > 0$. Starting from $T_{\text{amb}} = 19.29^\circ\text{C}$, the casing \linelabel{ln:trial2-ss}heated continuously during the active stall phase ($t \in [0, 472]\text{ s}$) and reached an asymptotic thermal plateau (steady-state maximum) of $T_{\max} = 70.21^\circ\text{C}$ ($\Delta T_\infty = 50.92^\circ\text{C}$) before the stall was released at $t = 472\text{ s}$, triggering exponential cooling back toward ambient.
		\item \textbf{Current Telemetry and Control Discrimination:} To determine whether the internal controller follows unconstrained bang-bang switching or proportional PWM with saturation, supply current was acquired at 20~Hz across the 5.0~V rail under three operating regimes: (i) small steps ($e = 5^\circ$) draw transient pulses ($< 50\text{ ms}$, peak $582.7\text{ mA}$) before settling into the quiescent dead-band ($I_{\text{quiesc}} \approx 3.12\text{ mA}$); (ii) large steps ($e = 90^\circ$) exhibit transients scaling with motor slew rate ($\Delta t \approx 150\text{--}180\text{ ms}$, peak $598.4\text{ mA}$); and (iii) persistent mechanical stall forces continuous 100\% PWM saturation, drawing $I_{\text{stall}} = 918.4 \pm 142.1\text{ mA}$ (peak $1796.9\text{ mA}$) and continuously dissipating $P_0 = 4.59\text{ W}$ in heat. While internal armature voltage was not directly probed with a high-bandwidth oscilloscope (a methodological limitation where bus current acts as an indirect proxy), the quiescent dead-band and variable transient duration conclusively refute unconstrained bang-bang control.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection{Thermal Modeling and Parameter Identification}
	\label{sec:thermal_modeling}
	
	\linelabel{ln:tm-io}To accurately characterize actuator thermal dynamics under persistent stall, heat accumulation is formulated using a lumped-parameter thermal network. Prior to model derivation, the following physical assumptions and variables are defined:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
		\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
		\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
		\item \textbf{Single-Node Lumped Capacitance:} The actuator is treated as a single thermal node with lumped thermal capacitance $C_{\text{th}}\;[\text{J}/^\circ\text{C}]$ and casing-to-ambient thermal resistance $R_{\text{th}}\;[^\circ\text{C}/\text{W}]$.
		\item \textbf{Casing Surface Measurement:} Casing temperature $T_s(t)$ is acquired at the outer housing via a calibrated LM35 sensor coupled with thermal compound.
		\item \textbf{Constant Ambient Baseline:} Ambient temperature $T_a$ remains constant throughout testing ($dT_a/dt = 0$).
		\item \textbf{Locked-Rotor Joule Dissipation:} Under persistent stall, angular shaft velocity is zero ($\omega = 0$), yielding zero output mechanical power ($P_{\text{mech}} = \tau_{\text{load}}\omega = 0$). Hence, all electrical power delivered is converted into internal thermal dissipation ($P_{\text{diss}}(t) = P(t)$).
		\item \textbf{Electrical Power Estimation ($P_0$):} Because armature winding resistance $R_a$ was not directly measured, input stall power is estimated as $P_0 = V_{\text{bus}} \cdot I_{\text{stall}}$ with $V_{\text{bus}} = 5.0\text{ V}$ and mean stall current $I_{\text{stall}} = 0.9184\text{ A}$ ($918.4 \pm 142.1\text{ mA}$). This provides an effective upper bound, as $P_0$ encompasses quiescent driver consumption alongside winding Joule dissipation ($I^2 R_a$).
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\linelabel{ln:tm-tf}Defining the thermal elevation as $\Delta T(t) = T_s(t) - T_a$, dynamic heat balance dictates that net stored thermal energy equals electrical power minus dissipation:
	\begin{equation}
		C_{\text{th}}\frac{d\Delta T(t)}{dt} = P(t) - \frac{\Delta T(t)}{R_{\text{th}}}.
		\label{eq:thermal_ode}
	\end{equation}
	Importantly, commanded angle $\theta_d(t)$ is not the direct thermal input; rather, $\theta_d(t)$ establishes persistent angular error $e(t) = \theta_d(t) - \theta_{\text{stall}} \neq 0$ in \eqref{eq:persistent_error}, saturating the internal driver and enforcing locked-rotor current $I_{\text{stall}}$ and constant power step $P(t) = P_0$.
	
	Applying the Laplace transform to \eqref{eq:thermal_ode} with $\Delta T(0) = 0$ yields the electrothermal transfer function:
	\begin{equation}
		G_T(s) = \frac{\Delta T(s)}{P(s)} = \frac{R_{\text{th}}}{\tau_T s + 1},
		\label{eq:transfer_function}
	\end{equation}
	where $\tau_T = R_{\text{th}}C_{\text{th}}$ \linelabel{ln:tm-K}is the dominant thermal time constant.
	
	\linelabel{ln:tm-time}In the time domain, the step response to constant stall power $P_0$ is:
	\begin{equation}
		\Delta T(t) = R_{\text{th}}P_0\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_T}\right) = \Delta T_\infty\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_T}\right),
		\label{eq:first_order_time}
	\end{equation}
	such that absolute temperature evolves as $T_s(t) = T_a + \Delta T_\infty(1 - e^{-t/\tau_T})$, with asymptotic elevation $\Delta T_\infty = R_{\text{th}}P_0$.
	
	\linelabel{ln:tm-lin}A logarithmic transformation of \eqref{eq:first_order_time} yields:
	\begin{equation}
		\ln\left(\Delta T_\infty - \Delta T(t)\right) = -\frac{1}{\tau_T}t + \ln(\Delta T_\infty).
		\label{eq:linearized_thermal}
	\end{equation}
	Equation~\eqref{eq:linearized_thermal} is evaluated strictly as a linear consistency check using the independently identified $\Delta T_\infty$, excluding the terminal singular boundary where $\Delta T(t) \to \Delta T_\infty$.
	
	\linelabel{ln:tm-ls}Thermal parameters were identified via Non-Linear Least Squares (NLLS) regression over the constant-stall heating stage of Trial~2 ($t \in [973.1, 1445.1]\text{ s}$, $N = 473$, $T_a = 19.29^\circ\text{C}$), which reached an asymptotic plateau at $T_{\max} = 70.21^\circ\text{C}$ ($\Delta T_\infty = 50.92^\circ\text{C}$) before cooling. Trial~1 was preserved strictly as an empirical benchmark of destructive failure at $t = 226\text{ s}$ ($T_{\max} = 64.84^\circ\text{C}$), as premature test termination precluded reliable asymptotic parameter extraction from that single run.
	
	\linelabel{ln:tm-fit}The physical thermal network parameters are governed by:
	\begin{equation}
		R_{\text{th}} = \frac{\Delta T_\infty}{P_0}, \quad C_{\text{th}} = \frac{P_0}{(dT_s/dt)_{t=0}} \approx \frac{P_0}{\beta}.
		\label{eq:thermal_parameters}
	\end{equation}
	Because tests were terminated at $T_{\max} = 70.21^\circ\text{C}$ to prevent structural housing deformation, the heating trajectory resides primarily within its initial transient, making asymptotic parameters ($\Delta T_\infty, \tau_T$) collinear. \linelabel{ln:tm-claim}Consequently, the thermal dynamics are robustly characterized by the empirical initial heating slope:
	\begin{equation}
		\left.\frac{dT_s}{dt}\right|_{t=0} \approx \beta = 0.2143\,^\circ\text{C/s} \quad (12.86\,^\circ\text{C/min}),
		\label{eq:heating_rate}
	\end{equation}
	with $R^2 = 0.9930$ and an RMSE of $1.18^\circ\text{C}$ across Trial~1. With measured mean locked-rotor power $P_0 = 5.0\text{ V} \times 0.9184\text{ A} = 4.592\text{ W}$, the effective thermal capacitance evaluates to $C_{\text{th}} \approx P_0 / \beta = 21.43\text{ J/}^\circ\text{C}$, and the initial compliance rate is $\beta / P_0 \approx 0.0467\,^\circ\text{C/(W}\cdot\text{s)}$. \linelabel{ln:tm-bb}At this rate, continuous locked-rotor stalls cause housing temperatures to exceed the critical polymer softening threshold ($> 65^\circ\text{C}$) in approximately $205\text{ s}$ from ambient, establishing the necessity of the $15\text{ s}$ software watchdog timeout.

	\subsection{Kinematic Modeling of the 2-DOF Robotic Unit}
	\label{sec:module_kinematics}
	
	\linelabel{ln:kin-intro}Each modular unit $m_i$ ($i \in \{1, \dots, 16\}$) of the SOFIA kinetic surface comprises a 2-DOF pan-tilt mechanism actuated by two orthogonal MG90S servomotors with joint angular coordinates $\mathbf{q} = [q_1, q_2]^\top$. In single-axis characterization experiments, $\theta \equiv q_j$ ($j \in \{1, 2\}$) denotes the coordinate of the actuator under test.
	
	To establish the analytical forward kinematics, a kinematic tree of reference frames is defined by the chain of coordinate origins $o_0 \prec o_1 \prec o_2 \prec o_3$ (global surface datum $o_0$, module mounting centroid $o_1$ at $\mathbf{r}_{0,2}^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, intermediate pitch gimbal $o_2$ rotated by $q_1$ around local $X$, and hexagonal facet end-effector $o_3$ rotated by $q_2$ around intermediate $Y$).
	
	The composite orientation matrix $\mathbf{R}_{1,3}(q_1, q_2) \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ of the facet relative to the module base is given by successive elementary rotations $\mathbf{R}_{1,3} = \mathbf{R}_x(q_1)\mathbf{R}_y(q_2)$ in \eqref{eq:composite_rot}:
	\begin{align}
		\mathbf{R}_x(q_1) &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & c_1 & -s_1 \\ 0 & s_1 & c_1 \end{bmatrix}, \label{eq:elementary_rot_x} \\
		\mathbf{R}_y(q_2) &= \begin{bmatrix} c_2 & 0 & s_2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -s_2 & 0 & c_2 \end{bmatrix}, \label{eq:elementary_rot_y} \\
		\mathbf{R}_{1,3} &= \begin{bmatrix}
			c_2 & 0 & s_2 \\
			s_1 s_2 & c_1 & -s_1 c_2 \\
			-c_1 s_2 & s_1 & c_1 c_2
		\end{bmatrix}, \label{eq:composite_rot}
	\end{align}
	where $s_j \equiv \sin q_j$ and $c_j \equiv \cos q_j$ ($j \in \{1, 2\}$) denote compact trigonometric shorthands.
	
	Defining the nominal unit normal vector of the facet in its rest frame as $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_0 = [0, 0, 1]^\top$, the instantaneous surface normal vector $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{2,3}^{(i)}$ expressed in module base coordinates and its closed-form inverse kinematics for target pointing direction $\hat{\mathbf{u}} = [u_x, u_y, u_z]^\top = (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}_{0,2}^{(i)}) / \lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}_{0,2}^{(i)} \rVert$ are:
	\begin{align}
		\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{2,3}^{(i)} &= \mathbf{R}_{1,3} \hat{\mathbf{n}}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} s_2 \\ -s_1 c_2 \\ c_1 c_2 \end{bmatrix}, \label{eq:facet_normal} \\
		q_2 &= \arcsin(u_x), \quad q_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(-u_y, u_z), \label{eq:ik_closed_form}
	\end{align}
	subject to the physical actuator limits $[q_{j,\min}, q_{j,\max}]$ as evaluated in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sofia_ik}.
	
	\begin{algorithm}[b]
		\caption{SOFIA Module Inverse Kinematics}
		\label{alg:sofia_ik}
		
		\begin{algorithmic}[1]
			
			\Require Module base origin $\mathbf{r}_{0,2}^{(i)}$, target coordinate
			$\mathbf{p}$, joint angle limits
			$[q_{1,\min},q_{1,\max}]$ and
			$[q_{2,\min},q_{2,\max}]$
			
			\Ensure Joint angles $q_1,q_2$
			
			\State $\mathbf{r}_{2,p} \gets \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{r}_{0,2}$
			
			\State $[u_x,u_y,u_z]^\top \gets
			\mathbf{r}_{2,p}/\lVert\mathbf{r}_{2,p}\rVert$
			
			\State $q_2 \gets \arcsin(u_x)$
			
			\State $q_1 \gets \operatorname{atan2}(-u_y,u_z)$
			
			\State $q_1 \gets \operatorname{clip}(q_1, q_{1,\min}, q_{1,\max})$
			\State $q_2 \gets \operatorname{clip}(q_2, q_{2,\min}, q_{2,\max})$
			\State \Return $q_1,q_2$
			
		\end{algorithmic}
	\end{algorithm}
	
	
	\subsection{Computer Vision Tracking and Dynamic Gesture Pipeline}
	\label{sec:vision_pipeline}

	To maintain classification robustness across varying orientations, the pipeline evaluates finger extension states via distal-to-wrist 3D Euclidean distances normalized by anatomical palm scale $\lVert \mathbf{p}_{\text{wrist}} - \mathbf{p}_{\text{MCP}} \rVert$, eliminating perspective shortening and depth distortion.
	
	Interaction intent is arbitrated by a dynamic gesture recognition engine (\textit{GestureDetector}) that enforces strict temporal and priority hierarchies between static postures and continuous motions. Standard static postures, including two-handed heart geometries, open palms, victory/scissors signs, and closed fists, provide complementary stationary commands.

	\subsection{Thermal Protection and Reliability Risk Mitigation}
	\label{sec:risk_mitigation}
	
	\linelabel{ln:wd}Based on the empirical heating rate ($\beta \approx 0.214\,^\circ\text{C/s}$) established in Section~\ref{sec:thermal_modeling} and real-time current telemetry via the INA219 sensor, software-level watchdog mechanisms are integrated into the SOFIA kinematic controller:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Command Saturation Limiting:} \linelabel{ln:wd-sat}Commanded angles are hard-bounded within the verified operational range $q_1, q_2 \in [19.3^\circ, 70.2^\circ]$ (mean: $47.9^\circ$) established in Table~\ref{tab:MG90s}.
		\item \textbf{Stall Timeout and Pulse Suspension:} Because standard RC servomotors do not accept direct torque commands via position PWM, persistent stall conditions cannot be resolved by reducing hold torque. Instead, if an actuator target remains static under continuous high-current stall ($I > 450\text{ mA}$ recorded by the INA219 monitor) or continuous deflection for $\Delta t > 15\text{ s}$ (corresponding to a predicted unmitigated temperature rise of $\approx 3.2^\circ\text{C}$), \linelabel{ln:wd-torque}the supervisory controller suspends the 50~Hz PWM drive pulses to that specific PCA9685 channel. Suspending the pulse command immediately de-energizes the internal H-bridge, rendering the output shaft mechanically compliant (free-wheeling) and eliminating Joule heating before surface temperatures reach the $55^\circ\text{C}$ safety threshold.
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{Kinematic Tracking Simulation}
	\label{sec:results_simulation}
	\linelabel{ln:sim}To validate the analytical kinematic model, a dynamic tracking
	simulation of the 16-module SOFIA robotic honeycomb array was
	conducted in a virtual 3D environment \linelabel{ln:sim-unity}using the Unity engine~\cite{unity2023engine}. The objective was to evaluate
	the coordinated orientation of the modules toward a moving target
	coordinate $\mathbf{p}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
	
	\linelabel{ln:sim-ik}For each module $m_i$, the inverse kinematics procedure computes the servo joint variables $(q_1,q_2)$ from the relative target position in accordance with the analytical formulation established in Section~\ref{sec:module_kinematics} (Eq.~\eqref{eq:ik_closed_form}), enforcing actuator saturation boundaries. \linelabel{ln:sim-alg}The complete numerical tracking procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:sofia_ik}.
	
	Figure~\ref{fig:sofia_simulation} \linelabel{ln:sim-fig}shows six representative frames
	of the simulated tracking process. As the target moves through the
	workspace, the modules progressively change their orientation,
	maintaining the direction of their normal vectors
	$\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{2,3}^{(i)}$ toward the target position. The sequence
	illustrates the coordinated response of the 16-module array during
	different stages of the target trajectory.
	
	\subsection{Functional and Performance Evaluation}
	\label{sec:functional_performance}
	
	Functional tests were conducted to assess the operation of the
	vision, communication, and actuation components. To validate the kinematic
	tracking performance promised by the technological differentiator, a formal
	tracking protocol was established across two motion regimes evaluated over
	$N = 10$ repetitions: (i) continuous sinusoidal palm oscillations ($f \in [0.2, 0.8]\text{ Hz}$,
	lateral sweep $\pm 25\text{ cm}$), and (ii) discrete angular steps
	($\Delta q \in \{5^\circ, 30^\circ, 90^\circ\}$) under 500~lux illumination.
	System performance is benchmarked using two core metrics:
	the joint angular tracking error $\varepsilon_q(t) = |q_j^{\text{target}}(t) - q_j^{\text{real}}(t)|$
	(evaluated via Mean Absolute Error and RMSE), and the total end-to-end
	latency $t_{\text{latency}} = \tau_{\text{vis}} + \tau_{\text{comm}} + t_s$.
	Here, visual landmark inference accounts for $\tau_{\text{vis}} \approx 30\text{--}35\text{ ms}$
	(MediaPipe operating at $\ge 30$~FPS), communication and queueing overhead
	contribute $\tau_{\text{comm}} \approx 12\text{--}18\text{ ms}$ over BLE, and
	electromechanical servo settling requires $t_s \approx 75\text{--}180\text{ ms}$
	governed by the nominal slewing speed ($0.10\text{ s}/60^\circ$ at 5.0~V).
	
	True joint angles $q_j^{\text{real}}(t)$ are measured synchronously by
	combining internal PCA9685 PWM setpoint logs with a 120~FPS high-speed
	camera recording optical reference markers on the hexagonal end-effector,
	yielding sub-degree angular ground truth ($\pm 0.2^\circ$). This optical
	telemetry directly corroborates the electrical findings of Section~\ref{sec:thermal_modeling}:
	under small tracking adjustments ($\varepsilon_q \le 5^\circ$), the MG90S
	modulates PWM duty cycle smoothly and settles into its $5\,\mu\text{s}$ deadband
	($I_{\text{quiesc}} \approx 3.1\text{ mA}$), refuting unconstrained bang-bang
	switching; conversely, under large steps ($\Delta q = 90^\circ$), the drive bridge
	saturates strictly during slewing ($t_s \approx 180\text{ ms}$) before resuming
	proportional linear regulation.
	
	\subsection{Limitations and Failure Analysis}
	\label{sec:limitations}
	
	The current system still requires trajectory-smoothing improvements
	during continuous hand tracking to reduce abrupt changes in joint
	targets. In addition, the simulated 16-module array does not by
	itself establish the physical performance of the complete multi-unit system.
	While unit-level validation confirms tracking protocol feasibility,
	future experiments will scale optical tracking to multi-module arrays to
	evaluate inter-module spatial coherence, bus contention under 32-channel PWM
	dispatch, and thermal accumulation during sustained continuous interaction.

	\section{Discussion}
	\label{sec:discussion}
	
	\linelabel{ln:disc}The experimental characterization of the MG90S servomotor and the kinematic tracking simulation of the SOFIA kinetic array provide key insights into the intersection between high-level human-robot interaction and low-level electromechanical constraints.
	
	In terms of actuator thermal dynamics and control analogies, \linelabel{ln:disc-bb}current telemetry under small ($5^\circ$), large ($90^\circ$), and stall conditions distinguishes the internal drive mechanism: rather than a pure bang-bang controller, the MG90S operates as a proportional PWM driver that enters hard saturation ($I_{\text{stall}} \approx 918\text{ mA}$) only when physically blocked. In this unmitigated locked-rotor regime, the continuous H-bridge saturation dissipates electrical energy as heat at a quasi-linear rate of $\beta \approx 0.214\,^\circ\text{C/s}$. \linelabel{ln:disc-degr}This steep thermal gradient directly degrades internal components, causing mechanical softening of plastic gear retainers and brush wear when surface temperatures exceed $65^\circ\text{C}$.
	
	When contrasted with established modular robotics literature~\cite{seo2019modular}, where actuators are typically idealized as unconstrained position or velocity sources, our experimental findings emphasize that thermal accumulation constitutes a primary failure mode in densely clustered kinetic arrays. \linelabel{ln:disc-wd}By integrating software-level watchdog monitors and stall timeouts into the kinematic dispatcher, SOFIA is expected to prevent localized thermal overshoots while maintaining continuous social interaction~\cite{fong2003social} within safe operating envelopes.
	
	Nonetheless, several methodological limitations must be considered. \linelabel{ln:disc-lim}The external LM35 sensor measures casing surface temperature rather than internal coreless rotor winding temperature directly; consequently, internal winding temperatures are expected to reach higher instantaneous values than the outer shell readings. Furthermore, while the external 50~Hz PWM command stream is fully observable, the proprietary internal feedback loop of commercial servos remains inaccessible to external instrumentation. As a result, the identified first-order electrothermal model operates as an effective macroscopic indicator of thermal degradation rather than a microscopic reverse-engineering of the internal drive electronics. In future work, directly isolating the internal motor terminals to measure the armature winding resistance $R_a$ via four-wire Kelvin sensing will allow decoupling copper losses from driver electronics dissipation, refining the empirical estimates of $R_{\text{th}}$ and $C_{\text{th}}$.

	\section{Conclusions}
	\label{sec:conclusions}
	
	\linelabel{ln:concl}This paper presented the conceptual design, system architecture, and experimental actuator characterization of SOFIA, an interactive modular robotic surface comprising sixteen 2-DOF electromechanical units. By coupling real-time computer vision (MediaPipe 3D upper-body pose and 21-point hand tracking) with distributed ESP32-C3 / PCA9685 motion controllers, the platform synthesizes intuitive kinetic responses to human non-verbal gestures. Experimental evaluation under persistent position error conditions demonstrated that unmitigated mechanical stalls \linelabel{ln:concl-runaway}lead to a sustained temperature rise with a critical heating rate of $\beta \approx 0.214\,^\circ\text{C/s}$ and casing temperatures reaching $70.21^\circ\text{C}$. Incorporating angular limits and thermal watchdog timeouts mitigates actuator burnout risk, establishing a robust foundation for the physical implementation and deployment of modular kinetic surfaces.
